## The Weight of a Name

The grandfather clock in the foyer chimed nine times, each note landing like a stone in still water. Yuki counted them, her fingers resting lightly on the polished mahogany banister, feeling the vibration travel up through her palm. Nine o'clock. Nine rounds. Nine was a sacred number in her grandmother's teachings — completion, the end of a cycle. She wondered if the Steward had chosen the hour deliberately.

The others gathered in the drawing room without being summoned. That was the first sign that something had shifted between them — the thin membrane of civility that had separated their private calculations was beginning to dissolve. Olú stood by the fireplace, turning a crystal decanter in his hands as if weighing it. Béa had positioned herself near the door, close to the kitchens, her chef's instincts mapping every exit. Tomas sprawled on a velvet chaise, but his eyes were too sharp for the posture he'd adopted. Raj leaned against the bookshelf, a leather journal open in his palm, though his pen hadn't moved in minutes.

The Steward entered through a door none of them had noticed before — a sliver of paneling that swung shut without a sound. He wore the same impeccable charcoal suit, the same silver pocket watch on a chain that caught the lamplight. But tonight, there was something different in his bearing. A certain gravity. The way a hangman might carry himself before a particularly interesting execution.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he said, his voice the texture of old parchment, "welcome to Round Five."

Five rounds in. Each of them had survived four trials, their pots now compounding at sixteen million apiece. The math was intoxicating — and unbearable. Sixteen million each, but only one would leave with the full vault. The Steward had made that clear on the first night, his words sliding through the dining room like smoke: *The tickets were rigged. Only one can leave with the full vault. The others leave with nothing but the memory of what they betrayed.*

They'd known it from the start. Knowing it and feeling it were different animals.

"The fifth trial," the Steward continued, "is a trial of the tongue."

Béa's head tilted. "We cook?"

"In a manner of speaking." The Steward smiled, and it was the first genuine smile any of them had seen on his face. It was not a comforting sight. "You will each prepare a dish — not for the manor, but for your fellow guests. The dish must contain a secret. A truth you have not yet spoken aloud in this house."

The silence that followed was thick enough to drown in.

"A secret," Raj repeated slowly. "And what constitutes a valid secret? Who judges?"

"You do." The Steward produced five small brass bells from his coat pocket, setting them on the sideboard in a neat row. "Each of you will taste the others' dishes. Each of you will ring your bell if you believe the chef has revealed a true secret — something hidden, something vulnerable, something that could be used against them."

"And if we ring the bell," Olú said, his voice flat, "we validate their secret. We give them points. We move them closer to the vault."

"You ring the bell, you acknowledge their honesty." The Steward's eyes swept the room. "And in this house, honesty is the rarest currency of all."

The tension cracked like ice. Tomas laughed, a short sharp bark. "So we must serve each other our weaknesses on a plate, and hope they don't stab us with the fork?"

"Precisely." The Steward inclined his head. "The kitchen is stocked. You have two hours. The trial begins when the first dish is served."

He left through the hidden panel, and the room held its breath until the click of the latch echoed.

Béa moved first, pushing off from the doorframe with a grace that belied her thoughts. "Well, mes amis. It appears we are to be intimate."

"Or to destroy each other tastefully," Yuki said, and there was something almost amused in her voice.

Raj closed his journal. "The trick is not in the secret itself — it's in what you choose to reveal. We each have a hundred secrets. We need to find the one that costs us nothing but looks like everything."

"That's a journalist's answer," Olú said, setting down the decanter. "Calculated. Safe."

"And what's a coder's answer?"

"To find the bug in the system." Olú's eyes glittered. "The Steward said the secret must be true. He didn't say it must be current."

Tomas rose from the chaise, stretching like a cat. "So we lie by omission? Give them old sins, keep the new ones close?"

"Si, exatamente." Olú nodded. "The trial is about what we reveal. But it's also about what we don't."

Béa was already halfway to the kitchen door. "I will cook something that tastes of memory. That is my secret — not what I say, but what the food says for me."

They dispersed, each to their private preparations, but not before exchanging glances that said more than words. The alliances of the dining room were shifting, reforming, fracturing. Raj had spent the last two days cultivating Tomas, the footballer's easy charm masking a predator's instincts. Yuki had drawn closer to Béa, the two women sharing quiet conversations in the garden that none of the men had been invited to overhear. And Olú — Olú watched all of them, his dark eyes tracking every interaction like lines of code, searching for the logic flaw that would let him walk out alone.

The kitchen filled with the sounds of preparation. Knives on wood, oil in pans, the hiss of steam. The manor's pantries were obscenely well-stocked, as if the house itself had anticipated this trial. Béa moved through the ingredients like a conductor through an orchestra, selecting, rejecting, tasting. She had chosen her secret within minutes of the trial being announced, and now she was crafting the dish that would carry it.

A secret must taste like truth, she thought. It must be bitter enough to be believed.

She made a cassoulet. Not the rustic dish she'd learned in her grandmother's kitchen, but an elevated version — duck confit, white beans simmered in stock, a crust of breadcrumbs browned under the salamander. The recipe was hers, refined over years, the kind of dish she'd served to critics who'd wept at the table. But the secret she folded into it was older than the recipe.

The secret had a name. Léo. Her brother, who had died in a kitchen fire that she'd walked away from. She'd been the one who'd left the gas on. She'd been fifteen, and he'd been twelve, and the family had never spoken of it again. They'd called it an accident. She'd called it nothing at all.

In the silent kitchen, with the smell of duck fat filling the air, Béa let the memory surface and pinned it to the dish like a bay leaf. She would serve them her guilt, and they would taste it, and they would not know what they were swallowing.

In the pantry, Olú was constructing his own confession. He'd found the ingredients for a Nigerian pepper soup, the broth bright with scent leaf and uziza, and he was building a lie disguised as a truth. The secret he would offer was real — he had, in fact, been the one to push his startup partner out of the company, taking the funding and the credit while his former friend spiraled into obscurity. It was a true secret, and it cost him nothing to tell. Everyone in this room had done something ruthless to get here. That was the price of the golden ticket.

What he would not tell them was the secret that mattered — that he'd sold his own mother's land in Lagos to fund his first venture, and that she'd died before he could buy it back. That was a wound he wouldn't expose, not for any prize.

The pepper soup came together in a fragrant pot, the heat climbing with each added pepper. He tasted it, adjusted, tasted again. Good. It was a dish that burned going down, and that was exactly what he wanted.

Tomas worked in the corner of the kitchen, ignoring the others with the practiced focus of an athlete before a match. He was making feijoada — the black bean stew of his childhood in São Paulo, the dish his mother had made on Sundays, the dish he'd refused to eat when he'd become famous, ashamed of the poverty it represented. That was his secret, and it was true enough. But the deeper truth — that he'd paid a teammate to throw a match, that he'd fixed a game to cover his gambling debts — stayed buried in the stew's dark depths, a secret within a secret.

The beans simmered, and Tomas watched the bubbles rise and break, and he thought about how easy it was to give someone a truth that fit in their hand, while the real truth stayed wrapped around your throat like a chain.

Yuki had chosen to make ochazuke — humble, quiet, a dish of tea poured over rice. It was the meal her grandmother had made on the nights she couldn't sleep, the nights the old woman's hands shook too much to hold a knife properly. The secret she would serve was that she had resented her grandmother for that weakness, had wished for a different family, a different life. It was petty and small and true.

What she would not say was that her grandmother had died alone, in a hospital room, because Yuki had been at a competition and hadn't answered her phone. That was the secret that lived in the rice, in the green tea, in the silent way she arranged the bowl.

Raj watched the others from the doorway, his journal tucked under his arm. He wasn't cooking. He had decided, within minutes of the trial beginning, that he would not participate in the way they expected. He was a journalist. His whole life was about finding the truth that others tried to hide. And in this trial, the truth was on the table — literally.

He'd noticed something in the Steward's phrasing. *The dish must contain a secret.* Not "a secret you have" — simply "a secret." The words were precise, deliberate. The Steward was a man who chose his language like a surgeon chose a blade.

Which meant the secret didn't have to be the chef's own.

As the others cooked, Raj moved through the manor, following the path he'd been mapping since the first night. The secret he was looking for wasn't in the kitchen. It was in the walls.

He found it in the library, in a book that had been hollowed out to hide a small leather journal — the previous Steward's record of the manor's trials, dating back decades. The handwriting was cramped, the ink faded, but the words were clear enough.

*The vault does not hold money. The vault holds a choice.*

Raj read the entry three times, then closed the journal and slipped it into his coat. He was no longer a contestant. He was a journalist, and he'd just found the story of a lifetime.

## The Cassoulet Trial

The dining hall had transformed. Where silver and crystal had once gleamed beneath the chandeliers, now five white-draped tables stood in a loose pentagram, each bearing a single covered dish and a single chair. The candles had been replaced with low-burning oil lamps that cast long shadows across the parquet floor, and the air was thick with the mingled aromas of the dishes that had been carried in from the kitchen.

The Steward stood at the head of the room, his hands clasped behind his back, his face unreadable as ever. The clock tower outside was silent, but the manor seemed to hold its breath, the walls leaning in like spectators at a duel.

"Welcome to the Cassoulet Trial," the Steward said. His voice carried no warmth, no menace — only the flat certainty of a man who had read this scene a hundred times before. "Each of you has prepared a dish. Each dish contains a secret — a truth you have carried, a weight you have dragged through the rooms of this house. In a moment, you will each taste the others' offerings. But before you do, you must declare your own."

He gestured to the tables. "When you stand before your dish, you will speak the secret it contains. You will do so aloud, before this company. And then — and only then — will the tasting begin."

Olú was the first to move. He crossed to his table with the measured stride of a man walking to a verdict, lifted the lid from his pepper soup, and let the steam curl upward like incense.

"My secret," he said, "is that I ruined a man to build myself." His voice was steady, rehearsed. "My partner, Ade. We built our first platform together, in a rented room in Yaba. He wrote half the code. I took all the credit. When the investors came, I made sure they saw only me. When the company sold, I made sure he got nothing." He paused, letting the words settle. "He tried to start over. He couldn't. Last I heard, he was driving a taxi in Surulere."

He lifted his chin, meeting each of their eyes in turn. "That's my secret. It's true. And it costs me nothing to tell."

Béa went next. She uncovered her cassoulet with the reverence of a priest revealing a relic, and the scent of duck fat and beans rolled across the room like a wave.

"My secret is named Léo," she said. "My brother. I left the gas on when I was fifteen. He was twelve. He died in the fire. I walked away — not from the kitchen, from the truth. I let my family call it an accident. I let them grieve a tragedy instead of a murder by carelessness." Her hands were steady on the rim of the pot. "I have carried that guilt for twenty years. I have cooked it into every dish I've ever made. Now I'm serving it to you."

She stepped back, her face a mask of composure, but her knuckles were white against the table's edge.

Tomas uncovered his feijoada without ceremony. The black beans gleamed like wet stones, and the smell of pork and garlic filled the space between the other dishes' aromas.

"My secret is shame," he said. "When I was a boy in São Paulo, this was all we had. Black beans and rice, week after week, while my mother worked double shifts at a laundry. When I became famous, I was ashamed of it. I told people I grew up middle-class. I told my teammates I'd never eaten a poor man's stew." He laughed, short and bitter. "My own mother, cooking this in her tiny kitchen, and I couldn't even admit she existed."

He stepped back, arms crossed. "There. That's my truth. It's small and it's ugly and it's mine."

Yuki approached her table with the delicate precision of a pianist settling at a keyboard. She uncovered the ochazuke, the steaming bowl of rice and tea, and bowed her head for a moment before speaking.

"My secret is resentment," she said. "My grandmother raised me after my parents died. She was a pianist too — a concert pianist, once. But her hands failed her, and she was left with nothing but me and her memories." Yuki's voice was soft, almost inaudible. "I resented her for that. For being weak. For being a burden. I wished for a different family, a different life. I wished she would disappear."

She looked up, and there was something raw in her eyes. "She did disappear. In a hospital room, while I was at a competition. I didn't answer my phone. They couldn't reach me." She smoothed her dress with trembling fingers. "That's my secret. I wished her gone, and then she was."

Silence fell over the room. Four secrets lay on the table like slaughtered birds, their wings still twitching with remembered life.

The Steward's gaze moved to Raj, who had not yet approached his table. His dish sat under its cover, untouched, and he stood beside it with his hands in his pockets, looking like a man who had already left the room.

"Mr. Sharma," the Steward said. "Your secret."

Raj smiled. It was not a warm smile. "I have no secret to offer."

The room shifted. Béa's eyes narrowed. Tomas uncrossed his arms. Olú's hand went to his chin, a coder's tic when the logic failed.

"The trial requires a secret," the Steward said. "Every contestant must offer one."

"Contestant," Raj repeated. "That's an interesting word. You keep saying it. But we're not contestants, are we? We're subjects."

He lifted the cover from his dish. Beneath it was a single sheet of paper, folded into a triangle, resting on a plate of white porcelain.

"This is my dish," he said. "It contains a secret. But it's not my secret. It's yours — the manor's. The previous Steward's."

He unfolded the paper. The handwriting was cramped, the ink faded, but the words carried across the room like a bell.

"'The vault does not hold money. The vault holds a choice.'"

The Steward's face, for the first time, flickered. It was barely a movement — a tightening around the eyes, a compression of the lips — but it was there.

"According to this journal," Raj continued, "the manor has run this trial for over sixty years. The golden tickets, the compounding prize, the promises of wealth — it's all a stage. The vault has never opened for anyone. It's not a vault of money. It's a vault of something else entirely."

He folded the paper and tucked it back into his coat. "So I'm not offering a secret. I'm offering a question: What does the vault actually contain?"

The room went very still. The oil lamps guttered. Somewhere in the walls, the manor's pipes groaned like a sleeping animal stirring.

The Steward looked at Raj for a long moment, and then he did something none of them had seen him do before. He laughed.

It was a dry, creaking sound, like a door opening after decades of disuse. "You found the journal in the library. Hollowed-out book, third shelf, left of the fireplace."

"Yes."

"I put it there."

The words hung in the air. Raj's expression didn't change, but his hand drifted to his coat pocket, where the journal rested.

"I put it there," the Steward repeated, "for the one who would look past the prize. The one who would ask what the game is actually for." He stepped forward, his shadow swallowing the light of the nearest lamp. "You've passed the trial, Mr. Sharma. Not by offering a secret — but by finding the one I've been hiding."

He turned to the others. "The dish trial is concluded. The vault will open tonight. But it will not open for all of you."

The clock tower began to toll.
